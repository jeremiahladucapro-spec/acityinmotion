Get involved

While many people care deeply about their street, few of them ever speak at a public meeting. It is usually not because they lack standing; it is because no one has explained how the room works. This page walks through it, step by step.

Start here — the research

Political scientists at Boston University coded thousands of individual comments at planning and zoning meetings across 97 cities and matched every speaker to voter records. What they found: the people who show up are older, more often male, longtime residents, and homeowners — and they oppose new development at rates far above the general public.

The follow-up study is the part that should change how you think about this. When meetings moved to Zoom during COVID, researchers expected participation to broaden. It didn't. The paper is titled Still Muted for a reason.

The room is not a representative sample of the community; it is a small, self-selected group. This means one person who shows up carries far more weight than he or she would expect. While that gap is a real problem, it is also an opening for anyone willing to attend.

Einstein, Palmer & Glick, Who Participates in Local Government? Evidence from Meeting Minutes, Perspectives on Politics (2018) — winner of the APSA Heinz Eulau Award. Follow-up: Still Muted, Urban Affairs Review (2022). Read the research →

How the brain in the room actually works

Public comment is a persuasion problem, and persuasion has been studied for decades. Almost none of that research makes it into advocacy guides. Here's what matters.

01

A number is not a story

Thomas Schelling wrote in 1968 that a specific person's death evokes sentiment and awe, while statistical death evokes almost nothing. Decades of research followed, and there's real debate about how strong the effect is — recent replications have found it smaller and less reliable than early studies suggested.

But the practical lesson survives the debate: a crash statistic gives a council member nothing to picture. Your account of the specific corner where your daughter was nearly hit gives them something they'll still be thinking about at dinner.

Lead with the specific. Use the number as backup, not as the argument.

02

Losses land harder than gains

Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory holds up across an enormous body of work: people feel a loss roughly twice as intensely as an equivalent gain. This is why the opposition at these meetings is so effective — they aren't arguing about traffic modeling. They're saying you are taking my parking space. That is a loss, and it registers viscerally.

Safety advocates usually argue the opposite way, promising a future gain: fewer crashes, more people biking. Gains are abstract and discounted.

Name what's already being lost. Not "this would make it safer" but "right now I can't let my kid walk to school — that's already gone."

03

Facts don't dislodge identity

Motivated reasoning is one of the most replicated findings in political psychology: when information threatens something central to how someone sees themselves, they scrutinize it harder and often leave more convinced of what they already believed.

“Cars are dangerous” sounds to a lifelong driver like you are the problem. Defenses go up and nothing after that lands.

Attack the design, never the person. “This intersection sets drivers up to fail” keeps everyone on the same side of the table.

04

The curse of knowledge

Once you know something, you can't reconstruct not knowing it. Engineers and planners say “road diet” and “LPI” without registering that half the room just stopped following. The listener rarely admits it — they nod and disengage.

The reverse is your advantage. If you're not a professional, you're the only person in the room who can say it plainly, and plain language is more persuasive to the other non-experts sitting there.

Say it the way you'd say it to a neighbor. Check the glossary tab so the jargon doesn't lose you.

05

People follow people, not arguments

Cialdini's work on social proof shows we look to others to decide what's normal, especially when we're uncertain. In a room where five people speak against something and nobody speaks for it, silence reads as consensus — even when the silent majority disagrees.

Bring one other person. Two voices on the same side changes what the room sounds like far more than doubling the strength of your argument would.

06

First and last get remembered

Primacy and recency effects are foundational memory findings: the beginning and end of a list survive; the middle blurs. In a two-minute comment following twenty others, the middle is gone.

Open with your one sentence. Close with your specific ask. Everything else is support.

The two-minute comment

Most public comment periods give you two minutes. Here's a structure that uses all of it.

1
Name and where you live. Ten seconds. Being a resident is your entire credential — you don't need another one. “My name is Jeremiah, I live on Lucile Avenue in West End.”
2
Your one sentence. Say the ask immediately. Don't build to it — you may get cut off. “I'm asking for a marked crosswalk at Lucile and Lawton.”
3
The specific story. Thirty to forty seconds. One incident, concrete detail, no generalizing. “Two weeks ago at 7:40am a car went past me while I was halfway across with my son. There's no crosswalk there and the nearest one is three blocks up.”
4
Name the loss. What you've already given up because of how it is now. “We drive four blocks to a school we should be able to walk to.”
5
One piece of evidence. One only. Local beats federal. “This corridor is on the city's High Injury Network.”
6
Repeat the ask and stop. Restate it in the same words, then stop talking. Ending early is more powerful than being cut off.

What quietly costs you the room

  • Listing every problem. Three asks is zero asks. Pick one.
  • Speaking for “the community.” Speak for yourself — it's harder to dismiss and impossible to contradict.
  • Anger at the people in the room. Staff usually agree with you and are constrained by things you can't see. Make them an ally.
  • Only showing up in opposition. Agencies hear from people almost exclusively when something is wrong. Thanking them when something works buys real credit.
  • Assuming one meeting settles it. Showing up three times makes you a known quantity. That matters more than eloquence.

Find your meeting

For most people, the hardest part is not speaking. It is finding out where and when to show up. Below is the path for Atlanta, followed by a method that works in any city.

Atlanta

Neighborhood Planning Units

Atlanta is unusual, and in a good way. The city is divided into 25 Neighborhood Planning Units — citizen advisory councils that make recommendations to the Mayor and City Council on zoning, land use, and planning matters. Mayor Maynard Jackson established the system in 1974 specifically to give residents a formal channel into city planning.

Membership is open to anyone 18 or older whose primary residence is in the NPU, plus anyone who owns property or runs a business there. There's no application. You live there, you're a member. Each NPU meets monthly.

Worth knowing: most NPUs still offer a Zoom option, and many meet in the evening. If you can't get to a room, you can often still speak. If you're unsure which NPU you're in, the city's NPU office can confirm it and tell you when they meet — (404) 330-6070.

One catch: plenty of addresses with an Atlanta mailing address sit outside city limits. If the property lookup doesn't return an NPU, you're likely in unincorporated DeKalb or Fulton — check your county's site instead.

Anywhere else — the method

Most cities don't have an NPU system, but every city has some version of this. Work down the list:

  1. Find who owns your street. City, county, or state DOT — this determines who you talk to, and getting it wrong wastes months. Your city's GIS or property map usually shows it.
  2. Search “[your city] city council district lookup.” Nearly every city has an address tool. Your council member is your most direct line.
  3. Search “[your city] neighborhood association [your neighborhood].” These meet monthly, are easier to speak at than council, and often carry real weight with staff.
  4. Look for the transportation or public works committee. Full council meetings are broad; committee meetings are where street decisions actually get discussed, and they're far less crowded.
  5. Check your state's open meetings law. Every state requires public bodies to post agendas in advance — usually 24 to 72 hours. Find where yours are posted and check weekly.
  6. Email before you attend. A short note to the council member's staff or the city's transportation department often gets a faster answer than a meeting comment, and it creates a written record.

Organizations worth following

These are the organizations where much of the research, guidance, and debate originates. If you reference one of them in a meeting, people will know the source.

NACTOStandards

National Association of City Transportation Officials. Their Urban Street Design Guide and Urban Bikeway Design Guide are the practical counterweight to older highway-oriented standards. Also republished the FHWA countermeasure documents after they were removed.

nacto.org →
ITEProfessional

Institute of Transportation Engineers. The professional home for the field — technical resources, local chapters, and the Vision Zero and Complete Streets councils. Local chapter meetings are one of the easiest ways into the profession.

ite.org →
Strong TownsAdvocacy

Chuck Marohn's organization, arguing that the way North America builds is financially insolvent, not just unsafe. Their "stroad" concept and Crash Analysis Studio have changed how a lot of practitioners talk about design. Local conversation chapters in most metros.

strongtowns.org →
Smart Growth AmericaPolicy

Home of the National Complete Streets Coalition and the Dangerous by Design report, which ranks metros by pedestrian fatality rates. That report is one of the most-cited documents in local safety advocacy.

smartgrowthamerica.org →
Vision Zero NetworkAdvocacy

Tracks which US cities have Vision Zero commitments and what they're actually doing about them. Useful for seeing whether your city's pledge came with a plan.

visionzeronetwork.org →
Dutch Cycling EmbassyInternational

A public-private network that exports Dutch cycling expertise. Their design principles, study tours, and free resources are the most direct route to understanding how the Netherlands actually did it — and it wasn't culture.

dutchcycling.nl →
CROWStandards

The Dutch technical platform behind the Design Manual for Bicycle Traffic — the reference NACTO's bikeway guide draws from. This is the source document for protected intersection geometry.

Daily reporting on street safety, transit, and transportation policy, with city-specific editions. Openly advocacy-oriented — read it knowing that, and it's the fastest way to keep up.

Read

A place to start. You do not need to read all of them; any two will change how you see the street outside your door.

Walkable City
Jeff Speck

The most accessible entry point in the field. Speck's "general theory of walkability" — a walk must be useful, safe, comfortable, and interesting — is a framework you'll find yourself using constantly. Start here if you're starting anywhere. The follow-up, Walkable City Rules, is 101 specific things a city can actually do.

Curbing Traffic
Melissa & Chris Bruntlett

Written after the authors moved their family from Vancouver to Delft. The argument isn't that fewer cars is safer — it's that it's better: quieter, more social, less stressful, easier on children and older people. The human case, not the engineering one.

Inclusive Transportation
Veronica O. Davis

A working transportation engineer on how the profession divided communities — and what repairing that requires. Rare in being written by someone inside the field who is direct about its history. Essential if equity is part of your work.

Engineering in Plain Sight
Grady Hillhouse

An illustrated field guide to the built environment — what every pole, box, marking, and drain actually does. Not an advocacy book at all, which is exactly why it's useful: it teaches you to read infrastructure. Good for anyone who wants to stop feeling like a tourist in these conversations.

Sustainable Nation
Douglas Farr

Urban design patterns aimed at the bigger question of how places get built sustainably at scale. Denser and more professional in tone than the others here — better as a second or third read than a first.

Building the Cycling City
Melissa & Chris Bruntlett

The Dutch blueprint, and the antidote to "it only works there because of the culture." Documents how the Netherlands deliberately reversed course in the 1970s — and what five North American cities learned from copying it.

Does your city have a bike rebate?

Many cities, states, and utilities now offer e-bike rebates, tax credits, or point-of-sale vouchers, and these are often worth several hundred dollars. Most people do not realize their city runs one.

Search these exactly — swap in your city and state
"[your city] e-bike rebate"
"[your state] e-bike tax credit"
"[your city] e-bike voucher program"
"[your electric utility] e-bike rebate"
site:.gov "[your state]" ebike incentive
01
Check three levels, not one Rebates come from cities, states, and electric utilities — and they often stack. Colorado residents, for example, can combine a state point-of-sale credit with a separate Denver city voucher.
02
Don't skip your power company Utility rebates are the most overlooked source by far. Many electric co-ops and municipal utilities fund them quietly with almost no promotion.
03
Ask about income-qualified tiers Most programs offer substantially larger amounts for households under a certain income threshold, or for those on SNAP, Medicaid, or similar programs. These tiers are frequently 2–3× the standard rebate.
04
Get on the notification list Popular voucher rounds sell out fast — Denver's have been claimed within minutes of release. If a program looks closed, sign up for alerts rather than giving up.
05
Check the retailer requirement Point-of-sale programs usually require buying from a participating shop, and won't reimburse an online purchase after the fact. Confirm before you buy.
06
Look for cargo bike bonuses Several programs add a separate top-up for cargo e-bikes, on the reasoning that they're most likely to replace car trips. Worth asking about if you're hauling kids or groceries.

One caution: we deliberately aren't listing dollar amounts here. These programs change constantly — funding rounds open and close, amounts get cut mid-year, and several well-known programs have been paused entirely. Any list we published would be wrong within months. Search using the terms above and read the official program page before you buy. City and state .gov pages are the only source worth trusting on current amounts.

✓
Bulb-out / curb extension

Widening the sidewalk into the parking lane at a crossing. Shortens the crossing distance and forces drivers to turn more slowly.

✓
Capital Improvement Program (CIP)

The city’s multi-year list of funded construction projects. If your street isn’t in the CIP, no amount of agreement gets it built.

✓
Complete Streets

A policy that streets should be designed for everyone using them — walking, biking, transit, and driving — rather than for vehicle throughput alone.

✓
Comprehensive plan

A city’s long-range vision for growth and land use, usually looking 5 to 20 years out. Individual projects are supposed to align with it — a useful thing to cite.

✓
Crash modification factor (CMF)

A number estimating how much a specific treatment reduces crashes. The technical backbone behind claims like "protected bike lanes cut crashes 53%."

✓
Daylighting

Removing parking near a corner so drivers and pedestrians can see each other. Often the lowest-cost fix for a dangerous intersection.

✓
High Injury Network (HIN)

The small share of a city’s streets where most severe and fatal crashes happen. In many cities under 10% of streets account for over half of traffic deaths, which is why the HIN is where money goes first. Ask whether your street is on it — it changes the conversation.

✓
Leading Pedestrian Interval (LPI)

A signal that gives people on foot a few seconds of walk signal before cars get green. Cheap, fast to install, and one of the most effective pedestrian safety tools available.

✓
Level of Service (LOS)

An A-through-F grade for how freely cars move through an intersection. Historically the dominant metric in traffic engineering, and a major reason streets got wider: improving LOS usually meant adding lanes.

✓
Micromobility

Small, lightweight vehicles: e-scooters, e-bikes, bike share. Usually under 20 mph and often caught between the bike lane and the sidewalk in both design and regulation.

✓
Modal share

The percentage of trips made by each mode — walking, biking, transit, driving. Cities track it to see whether investment is shifting behavior.

✓
MPO

Metropolitan Planning Organization — the regional body that allocates federal transportation dollars across a metro area. In metro Atlanta this is the Atlanta Regional Commission (ARC).

✓
Multimodal

Serving more than one way of traveling. When a plan calls itself multimodal, ask which modes actually got space — the word is often applied generously.

✓
Protected bike lane

A bike lane physically separated from traffic by bollards, planters, parked cars, or a curb. The distinction from a painted lane matters enormously — protection is what changes who is willing to ride.

✓
Proven Safety Countermeasures

FHWA’s list of treatments with strong evidence behind them. Long used to settle arguments in public meetings — though five measures were quietly removed in July 2026.

✓
Public comment period

The portion of a meeting where residents can speak, usually limited to two or three minutes each. Often near the beginning; check the agenda so you don’t miss it.

✓
Rezoning

Changing what can legally be built on a piece of land. The most common reason a planning meeting gets crowded.

✓
Right-of-way (ROW)

The full width of public land from property line to property line — not just the pavement. Everything on the street competes for this fixed number of feet. When someone says "there’s no room," this is what they mean.

✓
Road diet

Reconfiguring a street to have fewer travel lanes — classically four lanes down to three, with a center turn lane. The space freed up usually becomes bike lanes, parking, or wider sidewalks. Also called a "roadway reconfiguration" when someone wants to avoid the word diet.

✓
Safe System approach

The framework underneath Vision Zero. Assumes people will make mistakes and designs the road so mistakes aren’t fatal — rather than trying to eliminate human error.

✓
Sneckdown

A snowy-day curb extension — the untouched snow at a corner showing exactly how much pavement cars never actually use. Informal, but persuasive.

✓
Speed safety camera

Automated enforcement that tickets speeders. Politically contested, but among the most effective speed-reduction tools measured.

✓
Traffic calming

Physical design that slows drivers — speed humps, chicanes, narrowed lanes, raised crossings. Distinct from enforcement, which relies on someone being caught.

✓
Traffic study

An analysis of how a project would affect vehicle movement. Worth asking whether it counted people walking and biking, or only cars.

✓
Variance

Permission to depart from zoning rules for a specific property. Frequently on NPU agendas.

✓
Vision Zero

A commitment to eliminate traffic deaths and serious injuries, built on the premise that these are preventable design outcomes rather than accidents. Originated in Sweden in 1997.
