Vision Zero

Vision Zero starts from a simple premise: traffic deaths are not accidents and not an acceptable cost of getting around. They are the predictable result of how we design streets — and design can change.

Part of my own work is contributing to Atlanta’s Vision Zero effort. This page is where I go deeper on what that work actually involves: what Vision Zero requires in practice, what Atlanta and other cities are trying, and what it takes to move from a policy commitment to a safer street.

01

Design for human error

People will always make mistakes. A safe system is designed so that when they do, those mistakes do not cost someone their life.

02

Speed shapes the outcome

Speed is one of the strongest factors in whether someone survives a crash. While speed is not the only variable, designing streets that encourage slower travel is one of the most direct ways we can protect the people using them.

03

Let the data lead

In most cities, a small share of streets carries the majority of severe crashes. Understanding where those streets are allows agencies to direct limited resources toward the places where they will do the most good.

Find your city's safety dashboard

If you are considering moving somewhere new, this is worth a look. Most cities with a Vision Zero commitment publish their crash data publicly, and how a city reports on safety says a great deal about whether it is investing in it. Search for yours below; it is one of the clearest windows into a city's priorities that residents have access to.

25 cities in the directory
AtlantaGA

ATLDOT compiles fatalities monthly from police, the medical examiner, and hospitals — not police reports alone. Filterable by injury, mode, location, and road ownership. 59 people died in 2024, down from a peak of 101 in 2021.

Open dashboard →
New York CityNY

Vision Zero View maps every injury and fatality to the nearest intersection, updated monthly, going back to 2014. New York recorded 205 traffic deaths in 2025 — the fewest since record-keeping began in 1910.

Open dashboard →
PortlandOR

PBOT tracks crashes, citations, and safety projects together. 39 people died in 2025, down from 69 in 2023. The High Crash Network — 30 streets and 30 intersections, 8% of the city — carried 67% of traffic deaths from 2020 to 2024.

Open dashboard →
SeattleWA

SDOT pairs collision records with traffic volume counts, so you can see exposure alongside crashes. 27 people died in 2025, and deaths have trended upward over the decade — the City Council requested a Vision Zero audit in April 2026.

Open dashboard →
BostonMA

Crash records from the city's 911 dispatch system, verified by Boston Police, updated monthly. Boston publishes individual fatality records rather than an annual total, and lets residents file street safety concerns into a public dataset. Targets zero by 2030.

Open dashboard →
San FranciscoCA

A traffic fatality notification dashboard with a monthly summary memo for every month, plus High Injury Network maps. 25 people died in 2025, down from 43 in 2024. The 2024 network identifies 13% of streets where 74% of serious injuries and deaths occur.

Open dashboard →
MinneapolisMN

10% of city streets carry 64% of fatal and serious injury crashes, measured 2020 to 2024. Minneapolis publishes the most explicit disparity data of any city here — Black residents are 19% of the population and 41% of people killed in vehicle crashes.

Open dashboard →
Greater Boston regionMA

Covers 97 communities — useful if you're looking at suburbs rather than the core city. The regional high-injury network is 7% of roadway miles and 65% of fatal and serious injury crashes.

Open dashboard →
AustinTX

The Vision Zero Viewer filters fatal and serious-injury crashes by mode, demographic, time of day, and location. Austin's ten-year review in 2025 showed progress on crashes and injuries, but not on deaths.

Open dashboard →
PhiladelphiaPA

A High Injury Network dashboard alongside annual fatality counts and program reporting. 80% of the city's traffic deaths and serious injuries happen on 12% of its streets.

Open dashboard →
San AntonioTX

The High Injury Network Explorer maps fatal and severe-injury hotspots down to the street, council district, and neighborhood. San Antonio adopted the first formal Vision Zero Action Plan in Texas in 2024.

Open dashboard →
DenverCO

DOTI tracks crashes and fatalities on a public dashboard. Denver refocused the program in a 2022 action plan after the original 2017 version failed to bend the curve.

Open dashboard →
ChicagoIL

Chicago publishes no polished dashboard — the open data portal is the real product. Every fatality counted in official Vision Zero statistics, mapped and filterable, updated monthly.

Open dashboard →
DallasTX

The Vision Zero data page carries both the High Injury Network map and a crash dashboard. 7% of Dallas roads account for 62% of severe and fatal crashes.

Open dashboard →
Miami-Dade CountyFL

The county runs Vision Zero here, not the City of Miami. 1,831 people were killed and 6,996 seriously injured between 2020 and 2025 — 40% of them on a bike, a motorcycle, or on foot.

Open dashboard →
SacramentoCA

A public crash dashboard covering 2013 onward with severity, contributing factors, and state-designated safety corridors. Unsafe speed is the leading factor in 15% of the city's severe and fatal crashes.

Open dashboard →
NashvilleTN

NDOT maps crash data alongside the High Injury Network. The dashboard is titled December 2021 and was last updated in 2024, so read it as a baseline rather than a current count.

Open dashboard →
CharlotteNC

CDOT publishes crash statistics and plan documents here; the city's Tableau dashboard was returning an error when we checked. The City Auditor issued a dedicated Vision Zero audit in 2025.

Open dashboard →
OaklandCA

Oakland runs Safe Oakland Streets rather than a branded Vision Zero program. Its High Injury Network map shows 8% of streets carrying 60% of severe and fatal collisions.

Open dashboard →
DetroitMI

All reported crashes from 2011 through 2024, with a density map, a time-of-day heatmap, and the ten worst intersections. 1,124 people were killed between 2016 and 2024.

Open dashboard →
MemphisTN

The MPO's crash dashboard recalculates fatalities and injuries for whatever part of the map you are viewing, which almost no city tool does. Filter by year, severity, mode, and alcohol involvement.

Open dashboard →
Las VegasNV

The city's own maps and data hub, distinct from Nevada's statewide Zero Fatalities campaign. Las Vegas committed in 2022 and targets zero traffic deaths by 2050.

Open dashboard →
PhoenixAZ

Phoenix folded Vision Zero goals into a Road Safety Action Plan backed by $10 million a year. Its High Injury Network story map covers 4,538 serious and fatal crashes from 2018 to 2022.

Open dashboard →
RaleighNC

Raleigh publishes its High Injury Network as PDFs rather than an interactive tool, so this points to NC Vision Zero's statewide dashboard — filter it to Wake County. The city targets halving deaths by 2040 rather than reaching zero.

Open dashboard →
DurhamNC

Crash data and High Injury Network mapping, published in English and Spanish. 10% of Durham's roads carry 74% of fatal and serious injury crashes — the tightest concentration in this directory.
